The Core Grammar of Price Action Before pattern names, there is a small set of recurring questions. Master these and most Brooks terminology becomes a description of the same underlying auction behavior.

  • State: trend, range, breakout mode, or transition?
  • Control: Always In Long, Always In Short, or genuinely uncertain?
  • Strength: what has each side actually accomplished?
  • Location: where is price relative to structure, magnets, extremes, and breakout points?
  • Attempt and response: what is price trying to do, and did it receive follow-through?
  • Failure and traps: who is now forced to exit or chase?
  • Trader's equation: does probability x reward justify risk?

CORE FRAMEWORK

1. Price action is participant behavior, not pattern decoration Price action is the record of a continuous auction. Every bar contains buyers and sellers, and every move is a claim about value. The useful question is never merely whether a bar is green or red; it is what participants attempted, whether the market accepted the attempt, and what the losing side must do next.

Read the behavior A bull breakout is a claim that the market is too cheap. A bear reversal is the opposing claim that the breakout has gone too far. Follow-through shows whether the market accepts the first claim. Failure creates trapped traders, and their exits can fuel the opposite move.

What to look for

  • Ask what traders expected before the move.
  • Separate effort from accomplishment. Large effort with little progress is information.
  • Track who is trapped in a trade and who is trapped out of a trade.
  • Treat labels as shorthand for behavior, not as causes.

Common error Pattern-first thinking: seeing H2, wedge, double top, or breakout and assuming the label itself creates an edge.

Memory hook ATTEMPT -> RESPONSE -> ACCOMPLISHMENT -> NEXT THESIS

2. Context means what the market has already proven Context is the accumulated evidence that changes the meaning of the next bar. It includes market state, Always-In direction, trend maturity, prior pressure, location, session phase, and recent successes and failures. The same signal bar can be excellent in one context and terrible in another.

Read the behavior A strong bear reversal bar inside a fresh AIL spike is usually a pullback candidate. The same bar after a mature wedge top, trend-line break, weak test of the high, and repeated bull failures can be a legitimate reversal trigger.

What to look for

  • Current market state.
  • AIL/AIS or uncertainty.
  • What the dominant side has accomplished.
  • What the opposing side has accomplished.
  • Where the setup sits relative to extremes and magnets.
  • Whether the move is early, mature, climactic, or already two-sided.

Common error Treating context as a vague feeling instead of an explicit ledger of evidence.

Memory hook CONTEXT = WHAT THE MARKET HAS ALREADY PROVEN

3. Market state: trend, trading range, breakout mode, transition Every bar and series of bars can be simplified into trend or trading range behavior, with breakout mode and transition as useful practical states. State comes before setup because the correct execution style changes with it.

Read the behavior In a trend, market inertia favors continuation and most countertrend attempts fail. In a range, neither side has durable control and most breakouts fail. In breakout mode, the correct stance is uncertainty until one side earns follow-through. Transition is the period when a prior trend is being damaged but a new trend has not yet been established.

What to look for

  • Trend: directional closes, urgency, shallow pullbacks, repeated with-trend success.
  • Range: overlap, tails, repeated reversals, failed breakouts, flat EMA.
  • Breakout mode: compression with credible potential in either direction.
  • Transition: meaningful countertrend accomplishment, but control not yet fully flipped.

Common error Using a trend setup in range context or fading a strong trend because price looks extended.

Memory hook STATE FIRST. SETUP SECOND.

4. Always-In direction: the control question Always-In is the answer to a forced-choice question: if you had to be long or short right now, which side would you choose? It is a control framework, not a requirement to remain positioned at all times.

Read the behavior AIL can persist through several bear bars if bears have not accomplished enough to flip control. AIS can persist while a trading range begins forming, but location becomes increasingly important as the range matures. A convincing flip usually requires a meaningful breakout or spike and acceptable follow-through.

What to look for

  • Who owns the most recent strong breakout?
  • Where are recent closes relative to structure and EMA?
  • Did the opposing side get follow-through?
  • Did the old side fail to resume after being tested?

Common error Flipping AIL/AIS because of every countertrend bar or refusing to flip after the market has clearly changed.

Memory hook CONTROL CHANGES ONLY WHEN THE OTHER SIDE EARNS IT

5. Strength, weakness, urgency, and effort vs accomplishment Strength is not a single large bar. It is cumulative evidence: closes near extremes, consecutive trend bars, follow- through, gaps, shallow pullbacks, successful breakouts, bad fills for traders trying to enter with the trend, and repeated failure of the opposite side.

Read the behavior A market can look violent because the opposing side temporarily disappears. That is a vacuum, not necessarily durable control. Conversely, a slow grind can be very strong when pullbacks repeatedly fail and countertrend traders cannot accomplish anything.

What to look for

  • Strength of closes and follow-through.
  • Depth and quality of pullbacks.
  • How much progress each new breakout makes.
  • Whether repeated attempts achieve less despite similar effort.
  • Whether traders are forced to chase or accept bad fills.

Common error Equating bar size with strength without considering location, maturity, and follow-through.

Memory hook ASK: WHO ACCOMPLISHED WHAT?

6. Follow-through is market acceptance The first breakout bar is a claim. Follow-through tells you whether the market accepts that claim. Strong follow- through raises the probability that the move is not merely a test or vacuum; weak follow-through makes failure and reversal more likely.

Read the behavior A huge bear bar in AIL does not automatically flip the market. If the next bar is weak or bullish and the bear attempt fails, the failed near-flip can become a strong continuation setup. Strong breakout plus strong follow-through justifies more aggressive entry; weak follow-through demands better price and more proof.

What to look for

  • Next-bar behavior.
  • Ability to hold beyond the breakout point.
  • Depth of the immediate pullback.
  • Whether the old side can quickly reclaim the breakout.

Common error Admiring a beautiful signal bar after the market refuses to confirm it.

Memory hook BREAKOUT MAKES THE CLAIM; FOLLOW-THROUGH EARNS BELIEF

7. Failure, trapped traders, and the fuel mechanism A failure occurs when traders reasonably expected something to happen and the market did not deliver it. The failed traders must exit; new traders enter to exploit the new information. That combination often accelerates the opposite move.

Read the behavior Failed breakouts, failed reversals, one-tick failures, failed wedges, failed final flags, and failures of failures are variations of the same behavioral engine. The important question is what was supposed to succeed and who is now trapped because it did not.

What to look for

  • Expected target that was not reached.
  • Breakout that could not hold.
  • Countertrend signal that triggered and immediately failed.
  • Second attempt that also failed.
  • Stop-run that reverses back into the original trend.

Common error Calling every loss a failure pattern. A useful failure requires a clear prior expectation and identifiable trapped participants.

Memory hook EXPECTATION -> FAILURE -> TRAP -> FORCED EXIT -> FUEL

8. Location, magnets, and unfinished business Location changes the trader's equation. A strong bull bar in the middle of a range is different from the same bar breaking above a major range after repeated failures. Magnets are destinations where business is likely to occur; they are not automatic reversal levels.

Read the behavior Prior highs/lows, breakout points, range boundaries, measured moves, EMA, trend lines, overnight extremes, prior-day extremes, and round numbers can all attract price. A near-miss can remain unfinished business and lead to another attempt.

What to look for

  • Distance to nearby support/resistance.
  • Whether price is entering or leaving a fair-value area.
  • Whether the magnet has actually been tested.
  • Response after the test: acceptance, rejection, or continuation.

Common error Shorting merely because price reached a measured move or buying merely because price touched support.

Memory hook MAGNET = DESTINATION FIRST, TRADE SECOND

9. Trader's equation: probability, reward, risk A trade is justified by its expected value, not by winning percentage alone. High-probability trades can tolerate smaller reward relative to risk; lower-probability trades require larger potential reward. The premise, target, stop, and management must remain internally consistent.

Read the behavior A 40% opening reversal can be attractive if the swing reward is many times the risk. If you enter for that asymmetry and then scalp out for a small profit, you destroy the equation that justified the trade. Likewise, a small countertrend scalp may win often yet still have poor long-run mathematics.

What to look for

  • Probability estimate based on context.
  • Structural stop distance.
  • Realistic destination before resistance/support.
  • Planned management: scalp, swing, or combination.

Common error Changing a swing into a scalp because the open profit feels fragile, or widening the stop because the trade is losing.

Memory hook PROBABILITY x REWARD MUST JUSTIFY FAILURE x RISK

10. Structural invalidation, stops, and position size The market does not know your entry price, breakeven point, or account balance. Stops belong where the trade premise becomes invalid. Your risk budget determines size, not the other way around.

Read the behavior If the correct stop is four times wider than usual, reduce size rather than moving the stop closer. A trade that tests your entry price is not necessarily failing; evaluate structure. If you would not enter the same trade now while flat, exit rather than anchoring to breakeven.

What to look for

  • Define invalidation before entry.
  • Use smaller size when structural risk is wider.
  • Do not move to breakeven simply because price moved slightly in your favor.
  • Re-enter later if the thesis becomes valid again.

Common error Using hope, breakeven obsession, or dollar discomfort as market structure.

Memory hook STRUCTURE DECIDES STOP; RISK BUDGET DECIDES SIZE

TRENDS STUDY MODULE T1

Trend versus non-trend A trend is a period in which one side repeatedly gets better outcomes and the other side repeatedly fails. The central practical distinction is not pattern name but whether you should trade with direction or fade extremes.

Read the behavior Strong trends create urgency, repeated bad fills for late entrants, shallow pullbacks, and failed countertrend attempts.

What to look for

  • Dominant direction and Always-In state.
  • Quality of pullbacks and opposing attempts.
  • Follow-through after with-trend and countertrend breakouts.
  • Phase: fresh spike, channel, mature trend, or range transition.

Common error Reacting to isolated bars without reference to the trend phase and cumulative evidence.

Memory hook TREND = ONE SIDE KEEPS WINNING THE NEGOTIATION

No-trade filters

  • Tight trading range / barbwire, especially near a flat EMA and middle of the day's range.
  • Countertrend trade in a strong trend before meaningful damage.
  • Middle-of-range setup with weak signal bar and poor reward to the nearest magnet.
  • Huge late trend bar chased directly into a measured move, range extreme, or mature climax without room.
  • Trade whose structural stop is unacceptable unless size is reduced.
  • Trade taken because you have not traded recently, are down on the day, or want to recover a prior loss.
  • Setup where your planned scalp/swing objective is not defined before entry.
  • Any trade when you have lost contact with the market and are reacting to a sudden large bar.
  • Any setup you cannot explain in terms of state, control, location, attempt, and expected destination.
